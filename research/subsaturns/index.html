Sub-Saturns

While hot Jupiters are mysterious, a subset of them – the sub-Saturns – are even more enigmatic. These are gas giants with masses less than Saturn, with some even as light as Neptune. These objects are thought to be below the mass threshold for runaway gas accretion, so how did they form?

One possibility is that these sub-Saturns are “hot Jupiters gone wrong”: they began as more massive giant planets but then lost a significant fraction of their mass. UCLA PhD student Grant Weldon’s simulations show that this is not only plausible, but can also solve other challenges with their formation, such as the long timescales required for them to migrate inward. We wrote a paper on the discovery of TOI-2195 A b, a planet only 1.5 times the mass of Neptune but with a radius close to Jupiter. Grant showed that this planet likely formed by losing up to 90% of its mass during high-eccentricity migration, and he is now turning this case study toward a broader population analysis.

Another line of evidence for a high-eccentricity formation history of sub-Saturns comes from the observation that many of them are on orbits highly misaligned with their host stars’ spin axes. This angle can be measured with the Rossiter-McLaughlin effect, an anomalous shift in the stellar radial-velocity as a planet occults parts of the stellar disk that are blue- or red-shifted by rotation. I have been leading a survey to measure sub-Saturn obliquities (Yee et al. 2025a, 2025c, Tamburo, Yee et al. 2025a, 2025b), with current results appearing to confirm a broad diversity in stellar obliquities. This work includes a close collaboration with Pat Tamburo and the Tierras team to measure stellar rotation periods for slowly-rotating stars and thereby infer true, three-dimensional stellar obliquities, making headway on the mysterious “preponderance of polar planets”.
